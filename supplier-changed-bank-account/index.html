Supplier Changed Bank Account? The Payment Diversion Scam When Sourcing from China

Short answer: if a "supplier" email asks you to pay a different bank account — especially a personal account or one in Hong Kong when your contract is with a mainland company — stop. Verify by phone through a channel you already trust before paying anything. If you have already paid, act within hours: request a bank recall, alert the real supplier, and preserve evidence.

This is one of the most damaging and most common scams in China sourcing, and it is growing as economic pressure pushes factories, and fraudsters, toward riskier behaviour. The pattern behind most of these losses is not sophisticated hacking — it is a compromised email box and an unverified bank-detail change that everyone was too busy to question.

A typical case

An overseas buyer places a USD 100,000 order with a factory it has worked with for two years. Mid-production, an email — from the factory's real address, in the sales manager's usual style — announces a "company account upgrade" and provides new bank details. The buyer pays. The factory later denies ever sending the email. The receiving account is closed. The cargo, if any, is held until payment disputes are resolved, and the buyer is meanwhile billed for port storage.

Variations we see include: payee names that differ slightly from the contract entity, personal accounts "because our company account is being audited", and Hong Kong accounts offered "for faster settlement". Each has a plausible story. All of them should trigger verification.

The red flags

  • Bank details change mid-order, close to a payment deadline — the moment you are least likely to delay.
  • The payee is a personal name, or a company name that does not exactly match your contract counterparty.
  • The new account is in a different jurisdiction (e.g. Hong Kong) from the contracting entity (e.g. a mainland Chinese company).
  • A new or unusual tone: urgency, secrecy ("don't call, our accountant is in a meeting"), or a sudden switch to a different email address or domain.
  • The email arrives right after a genuine conversation about an upcoming payment milestone.

The 10-minute verification protocol

  1. Never use contact details from the change-notice email itself. Call the number in your original contract, your proforma invoice, or the company's official registration record.
  2. Ask a specific person you have dealt with to confirm, in writing afterwards, that the change is genuine, and confirm the account name matches the contracting entity exactly.
  3. For first-time or large payments, make a small test transfer first and confirm receipt by phone before sending the balance.
  4. Check basic corporate records: is the company still operating normally, and does it have litigation or enforcement history suggesting financial distress? A supplier due diligence report covering registration, litigation and enforcement history is available within about five working days.

If you have already paid: act in the first hours

  1. Contact your own bank immediately and request a payment recall. Speed is decisive — funds still in the receiving account can often be frozen; funds moved on are far harder to trace.
  2. Contact the real supplier through the original channel to establish whether their email was compromised and whether the underlying order is real.
  3. Preserve everything: emails with full headers, invoices, transfer slips, chat records. Do not confront the suspected fraudster in a way that tips them off.
  4. Obtain bilingual legal support: a demand letter can be issued within 48 hours, and options include negotiation, police reporting in China, and civil recovery actions.

Prevention that actually works

  • Put a written rule in your purchase contract: any change of bank details requires written confirmation signed/stamped by the company plus verbal confirmation, and the company bears the risk of unverified changes.
  • Keep payment milestones tied to verifiable events (inspection passed, bill of lading issued) rather than calendar dates alone.
  • Segregate duties: the person who confirms banking changes should not be the person who initiates the transfer.

Frequently asked questions

Is an email from my supplier asking to pay a new bank account always a scam?

Not always — companies do legitimately change banks — but it is one of the most common fraud patterns in China trade. Treat any bank-detail change as unverified until confirmed through a channel you already trust, such as a phone call to a number from the original contract or a verified company registration record.

What should I do first if I already paid to a fraudulent account?

Act within hours, not days: (1) contact your own bank immediately and request a payment recall; (2) contact the real supplier through the original channel to confirm whether they sent the change notice; (3) preserve all emails, invoices and transfer slips as evidence; and (4) obtain legal advice on demand letters and cross-border recovery options.

Why do these scams succeed so often?

Fraudsters typically compromise a company email box through phishing and then watch real order conversations, so their fake bank-change emails arrive at exactly the right moment, reference real order details, and look completely routine. Verification by callback breaks the scheme, which is why a written internal rule against paying any new account without verbal confirmation is so effective.

Can I recover money paid to a fraudster's account in China?

Recovery is possible but time-critical: funds that are still in the receiving account can often be frozen through a bank recall and, in China, police reporting, while transferred-on funds are much harder to trace. Prompt action materially improves recovery prospects, and bilingual demand letters and negotiation support are available from counsel familiar with cross-border payment fraud.
