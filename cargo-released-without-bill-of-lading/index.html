Cargo Released Without the Original Bill of Lading: A Claims Guide for Foreign Buyers

Short answer: if your goods were released in China without you presenting an original bill of lading, you very likely have a claim against the carrier — and time matters. Preserve the evidence immediately, check the limitation period (generally one year under Chinese maritime law), and seek bilingual maritime counsel before the carrier's position hardens.

Release without the original bill of lading is one of the most consequential — and most misunderstood — disputes in China trade. Buyers often discover it weeks late: the supplier says "goods collected", the carrier says "delivery completed", and the buyer is still holding a full set of original bills that were never presented.

Why this is the carrier's problem

A bill of lading does three jobs: it is a receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and — critically — a document of title. Whoever lawfully holds the original bills controls the right to delivery. A carrier that delivers cargo without collecting an original bill takes on the risk of paying twice: once to the party that received the goods, and again to the lawful holder of the bills.

This is why carriers are not supposed to release cargo merely against a copy of the bill, a telex release without proper safeguards, or a "letter of indemnity" from a party that is not the bill holder. If that is what happened in your case, liability is usually clear; the real questions become who to sue, where, and for how much.

Step 1: Confirm what actually happened

  • Do you still hold the full set of original bills of lading?
  • What does the carrier's delivery record say — who collected the cargo, against what document, and on what date?
  • Was the "release" actually a telex-release / express-release arrangement, and did you authorise it?

If you hold original bills and never authorised release, document that immediately.

Step 2: Preserve evidence now

  • The full set of original bills of lading — keep them safe; they are central evidence.
  • The sales contract, commercial invoice, packing list and payment records.
  • All correspondence with the carrier, forwarder and supplier about delivery and release.
  • Evidence of the goods' value: invoice, customs declaration, or a survey report if quality/value is disputed.

Do not send originals to anyone, do not surrender the bills in exchange for promises, and be careful about signing any "settlement" or "confirmation" document the carrier's agent may put in front of you at the port.

Step 3: Identify the right defendant

Claims are typically brought against the carrier that authorised the release. Depending on the documents, the issuing freight forwarder or NVOCC may also be liable. The party that collected the goods can sometimes be pursued as well, but such parties are often judgment-proof. Which combination of defendants offers the best recovery prospects depends on where the release occurred, the applicable ticket terms, and where the defendants' assets are — this is a case-specific assessment.

Step 4: Watch the clock

Claims arising from carriage of goods by sea are generally subject to a one-year limitation period under Chinese maritime law, running from when you knew or should have known of the loss. The starting point and any interruptions are fact-specific, so do not assume you have a comfortable year — obtain advice promptly.

Step 5: Demand, negotiate, litigate

  1. Formal bilingual demand to the carrier with a clear statement of liability and a deadline. A demand letter can be issued within 48 hours of engagement.
  2. Negotiation / mediation — many of these disputes settle once the carrier's liability is clear and evidence is preserved.
  3. Litigation or arbitration — Chinese maritime courts handle these claims, and foreign arbitral awards are recognised and enforced in China under the New York Convention. Representation can be quoted per procedural stage.

Prevention for your next shipment

  • When payment security matters, use a negotiable bill of lading — not a sea waybill — and keep control of the originals until payment terms are met.
  • Consider letters of credit or inspection-linked payment milestones for new suppliers.
  • Scrutinise any request from the supplier to "switch to telex release": it is legitimate in many trades, but it is also how bill control is quietly lost.

Frequently asked questions

What is release of cargo without the original bill of lading?

It occurs when the carrier or its agent delivers the goods at the destination port without the consignee presenting an original, duly issued bill of lading. Under Chinese maritime law the bill of lading functions as a document of title, so delivery without it exposes the carrier to liability even if the receiver appeared legitimate.

Who can I claim against: the carrier, the freight forwarder, or the person who took the goods?

Claims are typically brought against the carrier that released the goods; in some cases the issuing forwarder or NVOCC can also be liable. The person who collected the cargo may be pursued as well, but they are often beyond practical reach. Which defendant is best depends on where the release happened, the ticket terms, and the defendants' assets — a case-specific assessment is needed.

How long do I have to bring a claim in China?

Claims arising from carriage of goods by sea are generally subject to a one-year limitation period under Chinese maritime law, running from the time the claimant knew or should have known of the loss. Because the starting point and possible interruptions are fact-specific, obtain advice promptly rather than relying on the full year.

What if I only hold a sea waybill instead of a negotiable bill of lading?

A sea waybill is not a document of title: the carrier is entitled to deliver to the named consignee without presenting any original document, so a claim for "release without B/L" will usually fail. This is precisely why buyers paying by open account should think carefully about whether a negotiable bill of lading, a letter of credit, or at least an inspection-linked payment milestone is appropriate for the transaction.

What is a realistic outcome: litigation or settlement?

Many release-without-B/L disputes settle after a formal demand and negotiation, particularly where evidence is preserved early and liability is clear. Litigation before Chinese maritime courts is available when settlement fails, and Chinese courts recognise and enforce foreign arbitral awards under the New York Convention. Quotes are provided case by case, and representation can be arranged per procedural stage.
